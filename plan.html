01 Concept

A word that means two things at once

In a model, parameters are the weights — thirty billion of them, of which about three and a half billion fire for any given token. In a life, parameters are the limits you work inside: the parameters of a conversation, of a job, of a self.

The show is about the second meaning arriving through the first. AI hands us a new vocabulary for what a mind is, and we don’t leave it on the machines. We turn it on ourselves — and the picture that comes back is not a single unified thinker. It is a committee, distributed across hardware, most of it idle at any moment.

You watch a router pick eight experts out of a hundred and twenty-eight, and you think: oh. That’s me, deciding.

None of this is speculative for you. It is already written down across your repos, and the show’s job is to make it physical:

  • consciousness-hacks already asks the question the show asks — is consciousness inherently biological, or could it exist in another substrate? That is the wall text, more or less.
  • pansophist rebuilds knowledge around the observer, on the grounds that treating facts as ownerless “is useful for encyclopedias — it is not how a mind works.” You have already written a theory of selfhood, in code.
  • distributed-70b splits a 42.5 GB model across two Xaviers, board one holding half the layers and streaming activations to board two over gigabit ethernet. One mind, two bodies, and a cable you can point at.
  • Mixture-of-experts. Eight of a hundred and twenty-eight specialists per token; a single cold token in colibri costs eleven gigabytes of random expert reads across seventy-five layers.

That last one is the load-bearing move. A mixture-of-experts model is not like a committee — it is one, and the show puts it in a steel box where a visitor can stand between the two halves of a single mind and see the wire joining them.

The competitive position is unchanged and if anything sharper. Hayes Valley will see plenty of diffusion prints this year. This is not a show of AI images. It is a show about what AI does to your self-image — and it is the only one in the neighbourhood with a 70B model running on two boards fifteen feet apart.

Springback survives as the title of the wire work — the machine still overbends by exactly the amount the world takes back. It just isn’t the whole thesis any more.

02 The space

A 20′ container is a corridor, not a room

This is the constraint that should drive every hang decision, and it’s the one most easily forgotten while planning on a screen. Standard 20′ interior runs about 19′4″ long × 7′8″ wide × 7′10″ high (a high-cube gives you 8′10″). Confirm the real numbers with HVAW before you cut anything.

The consequence: nobody can stand more than about seven feet from a long wall, and with visitors in the box, realistically three or four. Long walls get read up close and obliquely, in sequence, as you walk. Only the back wall has a genuine sightline — a nineteen-foot approach that starts the moment someone steps through the doors.

Wall assignments

  • Left long wall — the mind. Four 27″ panels hung portrait in plasma-cut steel surrounds you fabricate, spread across fifteen feet at 57″ centre. A Xavier rides in the outer two frames, so the ethernet between them runs thirteen feet in the open. See section 07.
  • Right long wall — the object wall. Physical work, deliberately not a second wall of screens: bent-wire sculpture on brackets, alternating with the cyanotype each piece cast. Object and its own shadow-image, adjacent. See section 05.
  • Back wall — the body. The wirebender on a black plinth, lit hard and alone, with a rack above it for what it makes. A plinth reads as gallery; a table reads as workshop, and at the end of a nineteen-foot sightline that difference is the whole register of the show.
  • Entrance. Title and one paragraph of wall text, eighty words maximum. People read it standing in the doorway, blocking the only door. Keep it short for circulation reasons as much as literary ones.

Keep a 36″ clear channel down the middle. Anything on the floor eats the only circulation you have, and the box comfortably holds eight to twelve people before it stops being viewable.

What you mount into

The gallery hangs wooden boards on the container sides. That kills the plan’s biggest unknown outright — you are screwing into wood, not trying to defeat corrugated steel — and it replaces it with a smaller, sharper one.

The boards are a band, not a wall. Anything hung outside it has nothing behind it.

Measure the top and bottom height of the board run on day one. That single pair of numbers now governs every hang decision in the show, and it is the one input the 3D model can’t guess for you. Monitors at 57″ centre need roughly 43–71″ of board behind them; 18″ flat work at 60″ needs 51–69″. A typical 4′ band clears both comfortably, a 2′ ledger strip does not.

Two consequences worth planning around:

  • The gap behind the boards is a gift. Furring holds them off the corrugation, so there is a cable void the full length of both walls. Route everything behind it. With four displays and two Xaviers, that void is the difference between a wall and a cable nest — and cable management is the thing that most reliably separates a show from a workshop.
  • Ask what the boards are rated to carry. Four 27″ panels plus mounts is fifty to seventy pounds hung off one board run. If they simply hook over cleats, spread the monitors along more of the run rather than clustering them, and put the Xaviers on the floor or a plinth instead of a wall shelf.

The boards cost you about two inches of width per side. It doesn’t bite — the clear channel still measures 6′8″.

Power is now an energy budget

Running off your inverter answers “is there power?” and replaces it with a harder question. Mains is a wattage constraint you almost certainly satisfy; a battery is a watt-hour constraint that decides whether you reach closing time.

At four hours a day, this is comfortable. A 2 kWh usable bank at 85% conversion runs 5.8 hours against a 4-hour day — 44% headroom, which is real margin rather than a number that merely clears. You need about 1.4 kWh of bank to cover the day at all, so you are carrying roughly 40% more than the minimum.

Four hours turns the energy budget from a design constraint back into a checkbox. Spend the attention on the machine instead.

That margin is worth having, because it absorbs the things that don’t appear in the table: a hot afternoon, a bender that runs more than you planned, a battery a few years into its life, and the UV panel if you decide to expose during open hours (running it continuously still clears four hours, but only by 48 minutes — so expose between sessions rather than through them).

The real logistics question is no longer runtime, it’s turnaround: 1.18 kWh has to go back into the bank between days. Over a multi-day run that is the thing to have a plan for, and it needs shore power somewhere — at home is fine, but then the bank travels with you daily.

Three things to check on the inverter itself before you rely on it. None are capacity questions, and all three still apply:

  • Pure sine, not modified sine. The switching supplies in monitors and Jetson bricks buzz audibly on modified sine, and some refuse to run at all. In a silent steel box, an audible buzz is a real problem.
  • Surge rating, not just continuous. Displays and stepper drivers both draw inrush. Stagger power-up rather than switching everything on at once.
  • Where it recharges. If there is no shore power anywhere on site, you are carrying batteries home nightly, and that is a logistics task nobody has scheduled yet.

You no longer need to cut load to make the day, so keep both Xaviers — the second one is doing real work on that wall. Hold “drop to one Xavier, play the sim pane off a Pi” in reserve as the lever to pull if metering comes in high, not as something to do pre-emptively.

03 The move

Let the machine make the show while the show is open

You asked whether you have enough art to fill the container. The honest answer is that you have more than enough — but the question dissolves entirely once the bender runs a production loop for the length of the run.

Each piece it bends gets hung on a grid rack on the back wall. Day one there are three. Day five there are twenty. The wall fills as the show runs, and someone who comes on closing day sees a different exhibition than someone who came to the opening.

Note what the four-hour day does to the rate: the machine bends only while you are present. That is the right call anyway — an unattended bender can jam, and leaving it running overnight would eat the battery several times over — but it means output is bounded at four hours a day, so size the rack for the run you actually have rather than for continuous operation.

You don’t need a finished body of wire sculpture. You need a machine that is still working.

This is stronger than pre-made sculpture on every axis that matters. It removes the inventory question. It puts the machine on stage instead of hiding it as a tool that made the real objects offsite. And it is the literal thesis — model to material, with the gap visible in the room.

It also de-risks the hardest task in the plan, provided you design the fallback rather than improvise it. If calibration doesn’t land, the machine sits as an object with its own MuJoCo simulation running on a panel beside it, and the rack holds whatever you bent by hand. Titled deliberately — the model, without the metal — that reads as a legitimate piece, not a visible failure. The difference is entirely whether you decided it on Aug 17 or discovered it on Aug 24.

05 Photograms

Shadow through steel onto sensitised paper

This is the strongest idea in the show and it should be built, not filed. A photogram is a contact image: the object’s own shadow at 1:1, made without a lens, a sensor, or a single line of code.

In a show about the distance between a model and its material, the photogram is the far end of the scale — no model at all.

It earns its place three ways.

  • It completes the causal chain. G-code becomes steel, steel casts a shadow, the shadow becomes an image. Four transformations, each losing a little fidelity to the one before it. That is springback happening four times in a row, and it is visible on one wall.
  • It sets up the show’s only real gradient. The screens carry light that was computed — cv-draw’s path tracer deriving radiance from first principles. The object wall carries light that simply fell. Same subject, opposite ends of the method.
  • It is the only new work you can reliably finish in two weeks. No vendor, no lead time, same-day feedback. A failed sheet costs twenty minutes.

Cyanotype, not the alternatives

Use cyanotype. It is iron-based, washes out in plain water, needs no darkroom (a dim room is enough), and tolerates sloppy handling. Buy pre-coated paper for this show — hand-coating is a week-three luxury and a week-one liability.

Lumen prints on expired silver paper are gorgeous and wrong for a deadline: hours to days of exposure, and they won’t fix reliably. Van Dyke gets you brown instead of blue and adds chemistry for no gain here. Decide once, in favour of the robust process, and spend the saved attention on the shadows themselves.

The fog problem, and the fix that’s better than sun

August in San Francisco is overcast. Sun exposure will be unreliable and, worse, unrepeatable — you cannot tune an image when the light changes between sheets.

Buy a 395 nm UV LED panel. Five to fifteen minute exposures, weather-independent, and identical every time. It also runs indoors, which means exposures can happen inside the container during the show — the object wall can accumulate exactly the way the wire rack does.

Two techniques — do both, lead with the second

  • Contact. Sculpture laid on the paper, diffuse UV above. Sharp, 1:1, essentially cannot fail. This is your baseline and your insurance.
  • Point-source projection. A single small emitter at a distance, so the shadow magnifies and goes perspectival — a six-inch wire piece throws a two-foot image, and the wire nearest the paper stays crisp while the far end blooms. Much more dramatic, and much more yours.

The physics that governs the second one: penumbra scales with source size. Use a single high-power LED, not the panel, and put it as far back as your exposure time tolerates. A big emitter close up gives mush. This is the one knob that decides whether the projections read as sharp or as fog, so buy one bare 395 nm emitter alongside the panel and test both on day one.

One practical trap: cyanotypes need a running-water wash, and the container almost certainly has no sink. Either wash offsite between sessions, or bring trays and jugs and treat the wash as part of the demonstration.

06 The prints

Your cv-draw prints don’t exist yet — and may not need to

Every one of the twelve cv-draw renders in out/ is between 1100 and 1600 pixels wide. That is a seven-to-ten-inch print at usable density. For a wall where people stand three feet away you want roughly 200 dpi, so a 24 × 36″ piece needs about 4800 × 7200 px — twenty to forty-five times the pixels you have on disk.

The good news is that it’s all procedural and resolution is a single constant per script (W, H = 1500, 950). Nothing is lost; it just has to be recomputed.

Measured, not estimated

I timed the path tracer on this box just now: raytrace.py 8 at 1100 × 700 took 8.1 s wall across 28 cores — about one second per sample-per-pixel at 0.77 MP. Scaling to 24 × 36″ at 200 dpi (34.5 MP, 45× the pixels) at the README’s 420 spp puts a full-quality render near five hours; at 300 dpi, near twelve. Either is one overnight job. Time is not the problem.

The risk is memory, not hours. And an OOM at hour four of an overnight run costs you a whole day.

The tracer is vectorised over every pixel at once, so array size scales linearly with resolution. At 34.5 MP a single float64 RGB buffer is around 830 MB and the renderer holds several simultaneously. This machine has 30 GB total but showed only ~11 GB available. Render in horizontal bands and stitch, or clear the box first — and prove it on day one with a single band at final resolution, not on day ten with the whole frame.

The photograms just retired this deadline

In the first draft of this plan, large-format printing was the hardest date on the calendar: three to seven days of vendor lead time, files out by Friday Aug 14, and a bare wall if you missed. Putting physical work on that wall instead removes the vendor from the critical path entirely.

So the honest recommendation is: show cv-draw on a screen, not on paper. It is computed light — a path tracer deriving radiance from first principles — and displaying it as emitted light rather than as pigment is the more truthful presentation, not a compromise. The cyanotypes give the show its paper.

If you still want two or three of them printed for material contrast, that is a real argument and the render numbers above hold. Just treat it as an optional order placed by Friday Aug 14, sized so that missing it costs you nothing.

And put the two Xaviers at opposite ends, with the ethernet run long, slack and lit.

This is the show’s single strongest physical gesture and it costs almost nothing: fifteen feet of cable, dressed deliberately rather than hidden. A visitor walks the length of one mind. Tidying that cable away into the raceway would be the most expensive mistake available to you on install day — so say it out loud to whoever helps you hang it.

One honest caveat: a live camera on the public changes what you owe people. Post a plain sign at the door saying the interior is on camera and nothing is recorded — and then actually don’t record. Run the detection live and keep no frames.

Portrait, in cut steel

Hanging the panels portrait and surrounding them in fabricated plate does more conceptual work than it looks like. Portrait orientation plus an ornate frame is the visual grammar of portraiture — it is how we have always signalled this is a likeness of someone. Applying that grammar to a router firing and a memory graph is precisely the show’s argument, performed rather than explained. Four portraits of a mind, and the last one is of the viewer.

It also solves a problem you didn’t raise: four bare monitors on a wall look like a trade-show booth. Four objects do not.

Plasma steel, or laser something else

These are not the same decision made twice. Plasma cuts conductive metal and cannot cut a hole much smaller than the plate is thick, so on 1/8″ stock the ornament stays bold and chunky. A laser cuts wood and acrylic with a kerf around a fifth of a millimetre, so the pattern can go as fine as you like.

Bold and industrial reads as equipment. Fine and ornate reads as a portrait — which is the whole argument.

That points at laser, and the weights point the same way:

The schedule decides this. Anything sent out for fibre cutting re-introduces exactly the vendor lead time the photograms just removed — and that trap has already tried to bite this plan once. Laser-cut ply or acrylic you can cut, hate, and recut the same afternoon.

One real cost to name: steel is what connects this wall to the machine. The show has steel wire being bent by a steel machine at the far end; steel frames tie the mind to the body materially. Wood breaks that link. You can turn that into an argument rather than a loss — warm organic material holding cold computation on one wall, cold steel making bent gestures on the other — but make it a decision, not an accident. A hybrid works too: ply frames with a steel inner lip, so the aperture the light comes through is the same metal the machine bends.

The 3D layout switches between all three so you can put them in front of the curator. Watch for one thing when you do: ply frames on ply mounting boards lose their separation — same colour family, and the frames stop reading as objects. If you go wood, either stain them well away from the boards or lean on the backlight to carve them off the wall.

Two things the cutting should do besides look good:

  • Vent the boards. A Xavier inside a steel surround, against a wall, in a sealed container in August, is a thermal problem. Put a denser run of cuts low and high on the two frames that carry boards, so the ornament is the convection path. Decoration doing mechanical work is the most satisfying kind.
  • Leave a way in. If a board locks up mid-show you need to reach it without taking the frame off the wall. Design that access now; it is invisible in the drawing and infuriating on day three.

Lighting behind the plate

Backlighting the frames is what makes the cutting pay off. Light behind the plate turns every hole from an absence into a source, throws the cut pattern onto the plywood as a halo, and separates each frame from the wall so it reads as an object rather than a panel stuck to a board.

Give each frame its own accent, matched to the pane it surrounds — blue for split, magenta for routed, green for observed, amber for present. The wall then reads as four distinct portraits at a glance, from the far end of the corridor, before anyone is close enough to read a word.

Cheap in every sense: a short LED strip behind each frame is about 8 W, so all four add roughly 32 W — the energy budget absorbs it without noticing. Run the strips off the same feed as the panels so one switch kills the wall. And keep the strip hidden behind the plate rather than visible at the edge; a glimpse of the strip itself undoes the effect instantly.

This is a second fabrication project in a fortnight that already has one.

Say it plainly: four cut-and-finished steel frames is real shop time — design, cut, deburr, finish, mount — landing in the same two weeks as bender calibration. The honest scheduling move is to hold the frames until after the Aug 17 gate, when you know whether the machine needs you. If it does, cut two frames properly and hang the other two panels bare; an asymmetry you chose reads far better than four rushed frames. And keep the finish cheap and fast — mill scale with a clear coat looks better than most paint and costs an afternoon less.

09 Risks

What actually sinks this

01

The board band may not reach your hang heights

Resolved from “can you mount anything at all” — the gallery’s wooden boards settle that. What’s left is narrower: if the band is a 2′ ledger rather than a 4′ run, your 57″ monitors and 60″ flat work won’t both fit on it, and you’ll be re-planning the wall on load-in day. Measure top and bottom on day one and set them in the 3D layout.

02

Cyanotype exposure is a feel, not a formula

Your first sheets will be over- or under-exposed, and the point-source projections will be soft until the emitter is small enough and far enough back. It is a fast loop — twenty minutes a try — but it is a loop, so start it Wednesday, not the following weekend. This replaced print lead time as the wall’s main risk, and it is a much better one to have: entirely inside your control.

03

Heat

A sealed steel box, roughly 400–600 W of electronics, and August. The Xaviers will throttle before you’re uncomfortable, so instrument it rather than judging by feel.

04

Recharging the bank between days

Downgraded — a 4-hour day clears comfortably on 2 kWh with 44% headroom. What remains is turnaround: 1.18 kWh has to go back in overnight, every night of the run, which means the bank travels to shore power and back. That is a small daily chore nobody has put on the calendar, and it is the kind of thing that gets skipped on day three. Meter the real draw this week so the margin you’re relying on is measured rather than assumed.

05

Springback calibration is genuinely unpredictable

It’s an empirical loop against real steel, and it is the only task here you cannot estimate honestly. Budget more than feels right, and hold the Aug 17 gate rather than letting it eat week two.

06

Scope, now that budget is open

“Whatever it takes” fails by letting you buy more than you can install. Six works do not fit. Four do. Spend the money on lead time and labour, not on additions.

10 Spend

Where money buys back time

Since budget isn’t the binding constraint, spend it exclusively on the calendar and on your own hands.

  • The photogram kit, ordered today. A UV panel, a bare emitter, and more pre-coated paper than you think you need. Cheap in absolute terms, and it buys you a whole wall that no vendor can make you late for. Over-buy the paper — running out mid-loop on Saturday is the failure mode.
  • A bought display and track system instead of fabricating mounts. You have exactly one fabrication project this fortnight and it’s the bender.
  • A spare stepper driver and a second spool of wire. A fried driver on Aug 22 with no spare ends the centrepiece outright. This is the cheapest insurance on the list.
  • A plug-in power meter. Costs almost nothing and converts your energy margin from assumed to measured. This is the only power spend the four-hour day still justifies — you don’t need more battery.
  • The touchscreen for entropy-brush, rather than engineering an input hack around a mouse.
